## The question

Singlet oxygen (¹O₂) is linked to stress signalling, but how can such a short-lived molecule change which genes are active? One clue is that singlet oxygen readily oxidises the guanine bases in RNA, forming **8-oxoG**. What does that oxidation do inside a living plant?

## What we did

In *Arabidopsis*, we made singlet oxygen in the cytoplasm in three ways:

- with the photosensitiser rose bengal in the light;
- by moving the *flu* mutant from dark to light;
- with dehydration and light together.

We then measured 8-oxoG, how well RNA could be translated, and transcriptome-wide gene activity. We compared the results with two drugs that block the cytoplasm's (80S) ribosomes, cycloheximide and homoharringtonine.

## What we found

- **8-oxoG builds up in living plants** whenever singlet oxygen is made in the cytoplasm.
- **The gene response looks like a translation block.** The transcriptomes from all three treatments overlapped significantly with those produced by cycloheximide and homoharringtonine.
- **Less translation means fewer brakes.** As 8-oxoG accumulated, RNA was translated less. Short-lived repressor proteins such as IAA1 and JAZ1 then disappeared quickly, broken down by the proteasome.
- **Hormone genes switch on without the hormone.** Genes normally held back by the short-lived repressors of jasmonic acid signalling were switched on by cycloheximide, rose bengal, or dehydration with light, even without the hormone itself.

## Why it matters

This is a new kind of gene regulation. By oxidising RNA, singlet oxygen slows translation across the whole cell. Genes held in check by short-lived repressors are released first, and the stress response begins. The damage itself becomes the signal. The idea runs through our later work on [where singlet oxygen comes from](/research/2022-singlet-oxygen-origins/) and [how a fungal toxin uses it](/research/2023-cercosporin/).
