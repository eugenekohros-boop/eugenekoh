## The question

Pollen, and the anthers that produce it, are essential for plant reproduction and for much of the food we grow. Yet the genetic machinery behind pollen formation and function is still only partly known, especially outside a few model species.

## What we did

We compared 16,904 RNA-seq samples from 90 plant species, from bryophytes (mosses and their relatives) to flowering plants. For every gene, we calculated how specifically it is active in pollen and anthers compared with other organs. We then used clustering and functional-enrichment analysis to sort those genes into families and biological roles.

## What we found

- **Shared and lineage-specific pollen genes.** Some gene families tied to pollen and anther development are conserved across land plants. Others belong to particular lineages only.
- **New genes, tested in the lab.** The pipeline turned up previously unstudied genes and predicted their roles. We tested those predictions on mutant plants, with genotyping and detailed morphological analysis.
- **Gradual changes in pollen form.** Comparing major plant groups revealed evolutionary patterns in pollen shape, cell wall structure, cytoplasmic content and apertures. Some groups differ sharply, while others change gradually from one to the next.

## Why it matters

Knowing which genes build pollen, and how they changed over evolution, is a foundation for plant reproductive biology. It is also useful for biotechnology, where pollen fertility affects crop yield and hybrid breeding. The cross-species gene sets from this study are a resource for future work in both areas.
