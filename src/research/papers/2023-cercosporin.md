## The question

*Cercospora* fungi cause leaf-spot disease in many economically important crops. Most of them secrete **cercosporin**, a photodynamic toxin: it absorbs light and passes the energy to oxygen, making singlet oxygen (¹O₂), which helps the fungus to infect. But what exactly does cercosporin do inside the plant?

## What we did

We followed where cercosporin goes in the cell and what it does there. We worked with a plant the fungus does not normally infect (*Arabidopsis*) and one it does (*Nicotiana benthamiana*). We measured photosynthesis, guard-cell membrane integrity, RNA oxidation, translation and genome-wide gene activity.

## What we found

- **It goes to membranes and chloroplasts.** Cercosporin collected in cell membranes in an oxidised state, and in plastids in a mix of redox states. How much it collected depended on ongoing photosynthesis. It behaved in the same way, and did the same damage, in both plants.
- **Photosynthesis collapses quickly.** Cercosporin rapidly impaired photosynthesis, as measured by Fv/Fm, NPQ and photosystem I.
- **The leaf's pores fail.** In the light, stomatal guard cells quickly became leaky, which changed how much gas the leaf exchanges with the air.
- **RNA is oxidised.** The singlet oxygen oxidised RNA to form **8-oxoG**. This slowed protein production and switched on the genes that are typical of a singlet-oxygen response.
- **Not everything needs light.** Some of the genes cercosporin switched on did not depend on its light-driven chemistry at all.

## Why it matters

Cercosporin does not hit just one target. It inhibits photosynthesis, directly oxidises nucleic acids and triggers complex changes in gene activity. Knowing the full set of ways it attacks the plant is needed to understand how Cercospora causes leaf-spot disease. It also connects to our work on how [RNA oxidation acts as a stress signal](/research/2021-rna-oxidation-translation/).
