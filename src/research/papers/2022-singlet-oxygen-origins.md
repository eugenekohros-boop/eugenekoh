## The question

Singlet oxygen (¹O₂) has an extremely short half-life, so it can only act very close to where it is made. Yet it is closely involved in stress signalling. In [our earlier work](/research/2021-rna-oxidation-translation/) we showed that its effects on gene activity are strongly linked to stalled ribosomes in the cytoplasm, caused by oxidised guanosine in messenger RNA. So when a plant makes singlet oxygen, where is it made, in the chloroplast or in the cytoplasm?

## What we did

We compared the transcriptomes of several situations that either produce singlet oxygen or disturb photosynthesis:

- the *flu* mutant, and feeding with δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), both of which disturb chlorophyll production;
- treatment with DCMU, and the *ch1* mutant under moderate light.

We then compared all of these with an "index" transcriptome produced by cycloheximide, which blocks protein production in the cytoplasm.

## What we found

- **Chlorophyll building blocks out of place.** When chlorophyll production goes wrong, in *flu* or with ALA, light-sensitive chlorophyll intermediates can build up in the **cytoplasm**. In the light they make singlet oxygen there, which oxidises RNA and switches on singlet-oxygen-responsive genes.
- **Chloroplast-side stress looks different.** DCMU treatment and *ch1* under moderate light gave transcriptomes that resembled each other, but did not switch on the singlet-oxygen gene signature.
- **A way to tell them apart.** Comparing a singlet-oxygen transcriptome with the cycloheximide index shows whether the singlet oxygen came from the cytoplasm or from the plastid.

## Why it matters

Many mutants and growth conditions are described as "producing singlet oxygen". This comparison helps work out where the singlet oxygen is actually made, and therefore how it is likely to signal. That makes such experiments much easier to interpret.
