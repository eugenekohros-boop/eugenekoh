## The question

Carbon monoxide (CO) is best known as a by-product of incomplete combustion, from industry and burning biomass. But living plants also produce CO, and in both animals and plants it acts as a **stress-signalling molecule**. CO released from soil and decaying leaf litter has been studied. CO coming from *living* vegetation has hardly been measured, especially in real field conditions.

## What we did

Over a full year in the field, we used **automated twig chambers** to measure CO production and flux from the needles of mature Aleppo pines (*Pinus halepensis*). Some trees went through the natural summer drought, while others were irrigated. Comparing them let us separate the effects of light, heat and drought.

## What we found

- **CO builds up in drought-stressed leaves.** The emissions tracked heat-driven biological production of CO, not photodegradation.
- **Two ways out.** In irrigated trees, CO escaped through open stomata. Droughted trees close their stomata to save water, but CO built up inside until it escaped anyway.
- **A clue to what CO is for.** The results support a role for CO in the heat-stress response, possibly by limiting damage from reactive oxygen species.

## Why it matters

This is one of the few field studies of CO coming from living trees. It suggests that leaf CO is part of how trees respond to heat and drought, not just a by-product. It also opens a new question: what mechanism lets living plants release CO? The study calls for more research on that.
