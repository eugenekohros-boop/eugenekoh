## The question

Singlet oxygen can harm cells directly, or push them into a cell-death programme. Does it matter *where* in the cell the singlet oxygen is made? And does singlet oxygen play a part in the cell death that happens when plants suffer acute water stress?

## What we did

We used two photosensitisers, dyes that make singlet oxygen when lit, which settle in different places in *Arabidopsis* cells. Rose bengal (RB) goes to the plasma membrane, and acridine orange (AO) goes to the vacuole. We measured cell death by ion leakage and tested whether the singlet-oxygen scavenger histidine could prevent it. We also followed the vacuolar protease RD21 and its inhibitor in the cytoplasm, AtSerpin1.

We then asked whether the same events happen under acute water stress. To detect singlet oxygen we used a fluorescent sensor (Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green), EPR spectroscopy and singlet-oxygen marker genes.

## What we found

- **Location matters.** Histidine prevented the cell death caused by AO, but not the death caused by RB.
- **The vacuole breaks open.** With AO, the tonoplast disintegrated, and RD21 formed a complex with AtSerpin1. With RB, the tonoplast stayed intact and no complex formed.
- **AtSerpin1 protects.** Plants overexpressing AtSerpin1 had less cell death, but only under AO.
- **Drought follows the same script.** Acute water stress made singlet oxygen accumulate, and the cell death that followed was blocked by histidine. It came with vacuolar collapse and the serpin–protease complex, and AtSerpin1 overexpression reduced it.

## Why it matters

Damage from acute water stress closely resembles vacuole-driven cell death, and singlet oxygen may play a role in both. The balance between a protease and its inhibitor emerges as a control point for that death, which could inform the design of more stress-resistant crops. The work led to a provisional US patent (2017).
